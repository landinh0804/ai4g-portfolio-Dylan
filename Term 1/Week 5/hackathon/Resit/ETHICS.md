# Ethical reflection, hackathon 5
Our data comes from one bank in Taiwan in 2005. The clients are between 21 and 79 years old, and 60% of them are women. There's no information about nationality or migration, so we can't say anything about those groups.
We checked if our random forest works the same for different groups. For women and men it finds about the same share of the people who don't pay (recall 0.62 for women, 0.60 for men), which is good. Between the age groups it's also quite similar (recall 0.58 to 0.65). The problem is clients under 30. They get more calls they don't need: the model flags 31% of them, but only 22% really miss a payment.
We also wanted to know if the model actually needs the SEX column. It doesn't. F1 was 0.529 with it and 0.526 without it, and the share of women and men who get flagged almost didn't change (women 28% to 27%, men 31% to 30%). So in real use we think SEX should be left out. AGE, EDUCATION and MARRIAGE are personal too, and we didn't check if they work as a hidden stand-in for sex. That's something we would still need to look at.
Both kinds of mistakes hurt someone. If the model misses a person (false negative), nobody calls them and their debt keeps growing. If it flags a person by mistake (false positive), they get a call they didn't need, and it can feel like the bank sees them as a bad payer.
The clients were probably never asked if their data could be used like this. The CC BY 4.0 licence lets us reuse the data as long as we give credit, but that's not the same as the people saying yes.
So what did we do about it? We checked the results for different groups, we tested the model without SEX, and we added a clear warning. The model should only be used to decide who to call and offer help. It must not be used to refuse credit, raise fees or pressure people. And because the data is from 2005, the bank would have to train it again on new data before using it.
